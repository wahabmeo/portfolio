    How the model works

    A set of coupled equations runs in simulated minutes. Oxygen drives mitochondrial ATP; when oxygen falls, anaerobic glycolysis consumes glycogen and produces lactate, lowering pH. Low ATP fails the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (Na⁺ and water enter, the cell swells) and the Ca²⁺ pumps (cytosolic Ca²⁺ rises). Calcium and reactive oxygen species damage mitochondria; past a threshold the mitochondrial permeability transition pore opens, which the model treats as the point of no return. Ca²⁺, ROS and leaking lysosomal enzymes damage the plasma membrane. Necrosis is called when the membrane fails or ATP stays collapsed after MPT.

    During ischemia, ATP breaks down to hypoxanthine. On reperfusion, xanthine oxidase converts it with the returning oxygen into superoxide, causing an ROS burst and a Ca²⁺ surge. This is why a cell that was still salvageable can die after flow returns.

    DNA damage stabilises p53, which drives BAX/BAK, cytochrome c release and caspase-9 → caspase-3. Apoptosis is called when caspase-3 activity is high and ATP is still available. If caspases are blocked, sustained death signalling diverts through RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL into necroptosis, a programmed death with necrotic morphology.

    Rates are tuned so that severe myocardial ischemia crosses the point of no return at about 30–40 minutes, in line with classic canine coronary occlusion studies. The numbers are teaching approximations, not measured values.
